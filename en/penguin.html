Penguin

Waddling on land, flying through the water. A bird with two faces.

Scientific name Aptenodytes patagonicus
English name King penguin
Class Sphenisciformes, Spheniscidae
Range Sub-Antarctic islands and surrounding waters
Length 85–95 cm
Weight 10–16 kg
Notes A seabird that lives in colonies. Recognized by the yellow-orange markings from chest to throat. Dives deep to catch fish and squid.

The Bird That Gave Up the Sky for the Sea

Though birds, penguins gave up flying through the sky and found their way in the cold sea instead. Over a long history of evolution, they turned their wings into sturdy flippers and chose to live making the vast ocean world their own.

On land, the way they walk a little clumsily, testing each step, invites a certain charm — but the moment they set foot on the water, their movement transforms. Roaming freely underwater as if released from gravity, they are filled with the very nobility of a "bird that flies through the sea."

A Body Built for Swimming

What supports their superb agility underwater is a beautifully streamlined body and an astonishing physical design. Unlike most birds, their skeleton is dense and heavy, serving as ballast when they dive deep into the sea.

Moreover, the dense plumage covering the whole body completely shuts out the cold seawater, while trapping air between the feathers to provide excellent insulation and buoyancy control. The work of the tail feathers and feet — reading the currents deftly and changing direction at will — is also an essential element that supports their swimming.

Living with the Flock

Living in flocks, sensing one another's presence, is another great characteristic of penguins. To protect themselves from the harsh environment and from predators, they form communities bound by firm ties and share their daily lives together.

Watching the flock preen leisurely on land, or dive into the water one after another in turn, you see a calm and orderly time flowing there. In the way they communicate by call, and nestle close to one another, you can sense a rich sociality and a strong bond.

The Season of Raising Young

When the season to welcome new life arrives, the penguins' lives become all the more close-knit and dramatic. In the way a pair cooperates to build a nest, carefully warms the egg, and raises the hatched chick with all its might, dwell deep affection and a sure bond.

The sight of the parents taking turns to hunt at sea and bringing back plenty of prey for the chick quietly conveys the preciousness of the work of passing on life. And the growth of the chicks, preparing to set out for the sea just as their parents did, leaves visitors with deep emotion and warm memories.
